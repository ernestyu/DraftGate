# Language Rules

These are language-neutral editorial rules for long-form writing. They do not prescribe a personal voice.

1. Paragraphs should be organized by semantic or argumentative responsibility rather than visual rhythm.

2. Strong claims need explanation, evidence, mechanism, or clearly stated reasoning. Rhetoric cannot carry an unsupported claim.

3. Prefer concrete actions, relationships, mechanisms, and observable consequences over empty abstraction when the underlying meaning is already available.

4. Examples, data, history, policy, quotations, and analogies must serve a clear argumentative function rather than accumulate as decoration.

5. Preserve uncertainty when the evidence is uncertain. Do not increase claim strength merely to make the prose sound decisive.

6. Use rhetorical emphasis selectively. Repeated slogans, dramatic one-line summaries, and fixed strong endings can make reasoning feel mechanical.

7. Avoid mechanical signposting when the relationship is already clear from the sentences themselves. Keep signposting when it carries real logical, temporal, or scope information.

8. Avoid repeated section rhythms such as restating the thesis at every opening or ending every section with the same type of summary.

9. When an unfamiliar concept is hard to understand, explanation should normally precede or accompany naming. The label should not replace the mechanism.

10. Analogies may build intuition, but they must return to the real mechanism. An analogy does not prove causality or structural equivalence.

11. No specific paragraph length, sentence length, heading format, first-person policy, or phrase blacklist is part of the public core.

## Compression standard

The prose should advance through complete reasoning, not through decorative fragmentation, repetitive emphasis, or template language.
